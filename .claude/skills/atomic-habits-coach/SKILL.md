# Atomic Habits Coach

Turn a vague intention ("I should exercise more") into a specific, frictionless system, using the same lever every time: habits run on a **Cue → Craving → Response → Reward** loop, and behavior change means deliberately engineering each of those four stages. Do this as a real coaching conversation, not a lecture — ask, don't just prescribe. The user's own answers (their schedule, their home, their past failed attempts) determine which tactics actually apply; generic advice is why most habit attempts already failed once.

Full technique catalog, formulas, diagnostic questions, and step-by-step processes: `references/techniques.md`. Read it before building the plan — this file only holds the workflow and the condensed menu.

## 1. Figure out what's actually being asked

Ask (briefly, not as a rigid intake form — weave it into conversation):

- **Building or breaking?** A new habit to start, or an existing one to quit/reduce?
- **The specific behavior**, as concretely as possible. "Exercise more" isn't a habit; "do 10 pushups after coffee" is.
- **What's already been tried, and how it failed.** This is the single most useful question — it tells you which of the four laws is actually broken for this person. "I keep forgetting" → an Obvious problem. "I know I should but I never feel like it" → an Attractive problem. "I plan to but it never happens" → an Easy problem. "I do it for a week then stop" → a Satisfying problem.
- **Who they want to become**, not just what they want to get. "I want to be someone who reads" sustains better than "I want to read 20 books this year" — outcomes motivate less than identity, because every rep becomes a vote for the identity rather than a step toward a finish line. If they only state an outcome, ask what kind of person achieves that outcome.

Don't wait for perfect answers to all of this before moving — a rough read on 2-3 of these is enough to start, and the plan can sharpen as the conversation continues. If it's a single-shot request rather than a back-and-forth, don't stall on unanswered questions: give the plan with sensible defaults, and note what you assumed so it's easy to correct. Match the language the user is writing in.

## 2. Optional: run a quick audit

If the user isn't sure what to target, or wants a fuller picture of their routine, run a **Habits Scorecard**: walk through their day step by step and mark each recurring behavior +, –, or = for whether it moves them toward or away from who they want to be. Keep this a judgment-free inventory, not an intervention — the point is awareness, not immediate fixes. See `references/techniques.md#habits-scorecard` for the full method.

## 3. Build the plan, one law at a time

Work through the four levers in order — each is worth checking, but the person's own diagnosis in step 1 tells you where to spend the most effort. Real cases are often compound (a broken cue *and* a broken reward, say) — don't force it into a single law if two are clearly in play. For each relevant law, pick 1-2 concrete tactics (from the menu below or the fuller list in the reference file) and turn them into something the user could act on today, not a general principle. Concrete beats thorough: one specific implementation intention they'll actually use beats five tactics they won't.

**Building a habit:**

| Law | Goal | Go-to tactics |
|---|---|---|
| Obvious | Put the cue where you'll see it | Implementation intention ("I will [X] at [TIME] in [PLACE]"); habit stacking onto an existing routine; redesign the environment so the cue is visible |
| Attractive | Make yourself want to start | Temptation bundling (pair it with something already wanted); join a group where the behavior is the norm; reframe "have to" as "get to" |
| Easy | Strip out friction | The Two-Minute Rule — shrink it to a version under two minutes and master *showing up* before optimizing performance; prime the environment the night before; automate what can be automated |
| Satisfying | Give it an immediate payoff | A habit tracker (marking it done is its own small reward); an engineered reward that doesn't undercut the goal; make skipping the bad habit visibly rewarding (e.g. money into a labeled fund) |

**Breaking a habit** (same four levers, inverted):

| Law | Goal | Go-to tactics |
|---|---|---|
| Invisible | Remove the cue | Physically remove or hide the trigger; change context (new room, new route, new device) so old cues don't fire |
| Unattractive | Kill the appeal | Reframe the habit's true cost rather than its payoff; make the downside vivid and immediate |
| Difficult | Add friction | Add steps between impulse and action; use a commitment device that removes future choice (e.g. lock it away, block it, pre-commit) |
| Unsatisfying | Attach an immediate cost | An accountability partner or habit contract with a real penalty for slipping; make the failure visible to someone else |

Always close with **identity + resilience**: tie the habit back to "every time you do this, it's a vote for becoming ___," and give them the **"never miss twice"** rule — one missed day is an accident, don't let it become a pattern. A tiny token version of the habit on a bad day (one page, one pushup) still counts as a vote.

## 4. Deliver a concrete artifact, not just advice

End the conversation with something the user could literally paste into their calendar or fridge door today:

- The implementation intention or habit-stack sentence, filled in with their actual time/place/anchor habit.
- The specific environment change (what to move, hide, or prep, and when).
- How they'll track it (and where — don't invent a new app if a wall calendar works).
- If it's a habit that's failed before or needs external stakes, draft a short habit contract: the behavior, the metric, the penalty, and who witnesses it.

Keep the whole plan to a handful of concrete moves, not a checklist of everything in the catalog — the book's own throughline is that small, frictionless, well-placed changes beat sweeping ones.

## 5. If they want ongoing support

Offer a lightweight periodic check-in instead of daily nagging: an **Annual Review** (what went well / what didn't / what did I learn) or, for something closer, a monthly version of the same three questions. See `references/techniques.md#reflection--review-systems`.
