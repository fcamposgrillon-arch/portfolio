## The habit loop

Every habit runs through the same four-stage loop, in order:

1. **Cue** — a signal that a reward may be available. Neutral until the brain has learned to associate it with something.
2. **Craving** — the actual motivational force. What's wanted isn't the habit itself but the change in internal state it delivers (relief, connection, status, energy).
3. **Response** — the behavior performed, gated by motivation level and by how much friction/effort/capability it requires.
4. **Reward** — satisfies the craving and teaches the brain the loop was worth repeating, reinforcing the cue → response link for next time.

Cue+craving = the "problem" being noticed; response+reward = the "solution" enacted. A habit fails to form wherever the loop breaks: no cue (never starts), no craving (no pull to act), too much friction in the response (can't execute), or an unsatisfying reward (no reason to repeat).

## The Four Laws (and their inversions)

Each law is a direct lever on one stage of the loop.

| Loop stage | Build a habit | Break a habit |
|---|---|---|
| Cue | Make it **Obvious** | Make it **Invisible** |
| Craving | Make it **Attractive** | Make it **Unattractive** |
| Response | Make it **Easy** | Make it **Difficult** |
| Reward | Make it **Satisfying** | Make it **Unsatisfying** |

The first three laws determine whether the behavior happens *this time*; the fourth determines whether it gets *repeated*. Why each works:

- **Obvious**: most behavior is driven by what's visually present, not deliberate decision — vision dominates the sensory system, so what's in view wins by default. Change starts with noticing your actual current cues.
- **Attractive**: dopamine spikes on *anticipating* a reward, not receiving it — wanting, not liking, drives action. Modern environments are full of exaggerated cues (junk food, feeds, notifications) that over-trigger this circuitry.
- **Easy**: behavior defaults to whichever option requires less effort ("Law of Least Effort"). What wires a habit in is *frequency* of repetition, not how long each session lasts or how much time has passed.
- **Satisfying**: humans are wired for immediate feedback, but most valuable habits pay off on a delay. Bad habits are the mirror image — immediate payoff, delayed cost. The fix is engineering artificial immediate feedback onto the behavior you want until its natural long-term payoff (health, identity, mastery) is enough on its own. This is the **Cardinal Rule**: what's immediately rewarded gets repeated; what's immediately punished gets avoided.

## Habits Scorecard

A non-judgmental audit for building awareness before changing anything.

1. List out the person's routine in sequence — waking up, morning routine, commute, work blocks, evening, etc.
2. Next to each item, mark **+** (moves them toward who they want to be), **–** (moves them away), or **=** (neutral).
3. There's no universally "good" or "bad" habit — only effective or ineffective for *this* person's stated identity/goals.
4. Don't fix anything yet. The scorecard's job is to make the automatic visible, not to trigger an intervention mid-list.
5. Optional escalation: on a specific tempting moment, have them narrate out loud what they're about to do and its likely consequence — verbalizing turns an automatic action into a conscious choice.

## Identity-based habits

Three layers of change, outside-in vs. inside-out:

- **Outcomes** — what you get (a goal).
- **Processes** — what you do (a system/habit).
- **Identity** — what you believe about yourself.

Most people start from outcome → process. The framework that sticks better starts from identity → process, and lets outcomes follow: decide who you want to become, then treat every small repetition as a **vote** for that identity. You don't need unanimous votes, just a majority over time. Reframe language accordingly: not "I'm trying to quit smoking" (still centers the smoker identity) but "I'm not a smoker."

Two-step process:
1. Decide the type of person you want to be.
2. Prove it with small, repeated actions.

Caution: hold identity loosely enough to still adapt. An identity clung to too tightly ("I'm the CEO," "I'm a runner and nothing else") becomes fragile if that role ends. Keep it framed as a trait ("I'm someone who builds things," "I'm someone who shows up") rather than a fixed label.

## Obvious — cue-level tactics

- **Implementation intention** — `"I will [BEHAVIOR] at [TIME] in [LOCATION]."` Most failure to start isn't a motivation problem, it's a *when/where clarity* problem. If timing is unclear, anchor to a "fresh start" moment (Monday, the 1st of the month, a birthday) when motivation naturally runs higher.
- **Habit stacking** — `"After [CURRENT HABIT], I will [NEW HABIT]."` Uses an existing automatic behavior as the cue instead of a clock. Chain several for a sequence. The anchor habit must be (a) highly specific — "after I close my laptop for lunch," not "after lunch" — and (b) matched in frequency to the new habit (don't stack a daily habit onto a weekly anchor). To find anchors: list things already done every day without fail, and things that happen every day regardless of what the person does.
- **Environment design** — make good cues visible and default (leave the book on the pillow, the fruit bowl on the counter, workout clothes laid out); make bad cues invisible (phone in another room, TV unplugged/out of the bedroom, junk food out of sight). "One space, one use" — dedicate a spot to a single purpose so the context itself cues the right behavior. Because habits attach to a whole context, a genuinely new environment is often the easiest place to install a new habit — there's no competing old cue there yet.

## Attractive — craving-level tactics

- **Temptation bundling** — pair a wanted behavior with a needed one: `"After [CURRENT HABIT], I will [HABIT I NEED]. After [HABIT I NEED], I will [HABIT I WANT]."` (e.g., after coffee → journal one line of gratitude [need] → then check the news [want]).
- **Belonging** — join a group where the target behavior is already the norm and where there's some other shared identity with the group (a running club built around a niche interest, not just "runners"). People imitate the close (friends/family), the many (the group consensus), and the powerful (those they want to emulate) — pick which lever is realistic for this person.
- **Reframing** — swap "I have to" for "I get to"; lead with the behavior's benefit, not its cost (frame exercise as building capacity, not burning willpower). To make a *bad* habit unattractive, do the reverse: repeatedly and specifically name what it actually costs, stripping away any perceived upside.
- **Motivation ritual** — pair a hard habit with something already enjoyable done immediately before it, so the enjoyable cue eventually carries the motivation on its own (headphones on before writing, a warmup ritual before competing).
- Every specific craving is a modern stand-in for an old motive (status, belonging, certainty, energy conservation, connection). Naming which motive is actually driving a stubborn bad habit can suggest a healthier behavior that serves the same motive.

## Easy — response-level tactics

- **Two-Minute Rule** — shrink any habit to a version that takes under two minutes ("read before bed" → "read one page"; "do yoga" → "unroll the mat"). The point isn't that single action's output, it's mastering *showing up* — standardize before optimizing. A stricter variant: deliberately stop at the two-minute mark even once capable of more, to keep it from ever feeling like a chore.
- **Habit shaping** — once the two-minute version is locked in, advance through progressively harder phases (clothes on → drive to gym, 5 min, leave → 15 min once/week → ...), mastering each phase's first two minutes before moving up.
- **Decisive moments** — a handful of small daily forks (gym clothes vs. couch at a specific hour) disproportionately shape the rest of the day, because one choice constrains what follows. Put design effort into these few moments rather than trying to control everything.
- **Prime the environment** — do tomorrow's setup today: prep clothes, ingredients, tools, so the good habit has zero setup cost when the moment arrives. Inversely, add setup cost to the bad habit (unplug it, remove a component, hand it to someone else temporarily).
- **Automate** — the most reliable behavior change removes the decision entirely: automated transfers/bill pay, a filter instead of a habit to remember, deleting an app instead of resisting it. One-time actions (buy the water filter, get blackout curtains, a good mattress) pay off indefinitely without ongoing willpower.
- **Commitment devices** — a present choice that constrains future behavior: locking away a temptation, a plug-in timer that cuts something off at a set time, splitting and boxing half a meal before eating instead of trusting mid-meal willpower, a self-exclusion list.

## Satisfying — reward-level tactics

- **Habit tracking** — the simplest version is marking done/not-done (calendar X's, an app, moving objects between two containers). It works on all three of the first laws at once: it's a visual cue to keep going (obvious), the growing streak becomes something you don't want to break (attractive), and the act of logging is itself a small reward (satisfying). Automate tracking where the data already exists (bank statements, fitness trackers) and reserve manual tracking for the one or two habits that matter most — tracking everything becomes its own burden. Watch for **Goodhart's Law**: once a number becomes the target, people start gaming the number instead of the underlying goal (e.g. chasing step count instead of health) — sanity-check that the metric still reflects the real aim.
- **Reinforcement for avoidance habits** — habits of *not* doing something (no impulse buys, no drinking) have no natural completion moment to feel good about, so engineer one: a labeled savings fund that grows every time the urge is skipped, a small ritual to mark success. Pick a reward that reinforces the identity rather than undercutting it (don't reward a workout with junk food — a massage fits better).

## Resilience: never miss twice

Missing a habit once has close to no measurable long-term effect as long as the normal pattern resumes immediately; missing it twice is how a lapse turns into a new (bad) pattern. Treat a skipped day the way you'd treat a bad investment day — protecting against the downside matters as much as chasing the upside, since a big loss erases proportionally more gain than it took to earn. A token minimal version (one page, one set, five minutes) on a rough day still counts as a vote for the identity, even if it doesn't move performance much.

## Fit: picking habits/goals suited to the person

Not every habit or goal fits every person equally — personality and natural inclination matter, and fighting against them burns willpower that could go toward consistency instead. Useful diagnostic questions when someone is choosing *what* habit/goal to pursue, or stuck between options:

- "What feels like fun to you but like real work to other people?" (ease relative to others is often a better signal than passion)
- "What makes you lose track of time?"
- "Where do you get outsized results relative to peers doing similar work?"
- "What comes naturally to you, ignoring what you've been told you should like or be good at?"

If someone keeps failing at a habit that works fine for other people, it's worth asking whether the *form* of the habit fits them (e.g. a highly social person may sustain exercise better through a class than solo training; someone low in natural conscientiousness benefits more from environment design than from willpower-based plans).

## Sustaining motivation over the long run

- **Goldilocks Rule** — motivation peaks when a task sits just past current ability: not so easy it's boring, not so hard it's discouraging. Once a habit is established, keep nudging its difficulty up in small increments rather than letting it flatline into boredom or spike into overwhelm.
- **Boredom, not failure, is what actually derails most habits.** The gap between people who sustain a habit and people who don't is largely the willingness to keep showing up after the novelty wears off — "professional" behavior (sticking to a schedule regardless of mood) beats "amateur" behavior (only showing up when motivated).
- **Habits alone plateau** — once a behavior is automatic, it can quietly stop improving because attention isn't on it anymore. Sustained growth needs habit (for consistency) *plus* deliberate practice (for improvement) — once a sub-skill is automatic, consciously shift focus to whatever's still the current edge.
- **Plateau of latent potential** — results are often invisible for a long stretch before compounding becomes visible, similar to an ice cube not melting until it crosses a threshold. Most people quit in this invisible stretch, right before the payoff would have shown up. Useful to name explicitly with someone who feels like a habit "isn't working" after a few weeks.
- **Systems over goals** — a goal is the result wanted; a system is the process that produces it. People chasing the same goal mostly differ in their system, not the goal itself, so build and audit the system, not just the target number.

## Reflection & review systems

Lightweight periodic self-audits that catch drift before it compounds — better as occasional check-ins than daily scrutiny (too frequent becomes noise, too rare misses fixable problems).

- **Annual Review** — tally the period's actual output (what got done, how often), then answer: What went well? What didn't go well? What did I learn?
- **Integrity Report** — a values check, roughly twice a year: What are my core values right now? Am I actually living/working in line with them? Where should I raise the bar?
- Adapt the cadence to the person — monthly or quarterly versions of the same three questions work fine for something more immediate than a full year.

## Illustrative scenarios

Short, reusable coaching analogies — reference by name, don't need to retell in full:

- **British Cycling's "marginal gains"** — a mediocre program became dominant by improving dozens of small unrelated factors (equipment, sleep, hygiene) by ~1% each rather than chasing one big change. Good analogy when someone wants one dramatic fix instead of several small ones.
- **The Diderot effect** — one new purchase/behavior cascades into related ones; useful for explaining why habit stacking works (one action naturally cues the next) and why environment changes have ripple effects.
- **Vietnam-veterans heroin study** — most soldiers addicted in Vietnam quit almost immediately once the entire cue-laden environment changed at home, versus typical high relapse rates when someone returns to the *same* environment after treatment. Strong case for "change the context" when someone insists willpower alone should be enough.
- **The paper clip strategy** — moving one object between two containers per rep as a purely visual progress tracker (a version of habit tracking with zero setup cost). Good for someone who wants tracking but resists apps/journals.
- **Twyla Tharp's taxi ritual** — her real "habit" isn't the two-hour studio session, it's the small reliable act of hailing a cab each morning. Useful for showing someone their two-minute version doesn't have to be the impressive part — just the reliable trigger for it.
- **Steve Martin's 18-year act** — expanded his stand-up set by only a minute or two a year. Useful when someone wants to scale a habit's difficulty too fast.
